    What I Expect From You¶

    These expectations apply to all lab members. They are meant to support your growth, not to micromanage it.

    Scientific integrity¶

    • Be honest about your data and results. Report what you found, not what you hoped to find.
    • Maintain a laboratory notebook, both written and online. A written notebook captures real-time bench observations, deviations, and decisions in the moment. An online record (GitHub, OSF, or shared project folder) holds annotated code, processed data, and version-controlled methods. Both are required. A notebook entry written a week after the fact is a reconstruction, not a record.
    • Proactively draft and update SOPs. If you develop or refine a method, write it down in a form that someone else can follow. If an existing SOP has a gap or no longer reflects how the work is actually done, update it. SOPs are living documents and everyone is responsible for keeping them current.
    • Pre-register your study designs on OSF before data collection begins.
    • Archive all data and code publicly at or before publication. No exceptions.

    Communication¶

    • Be proactive. If you are stuck, behind, or uncertain, say so early.
    • Honor your commitments. If you agreed to deliver something, deliver it. If circumstances change, communicate before the deadline, not after.
    • Respect your colleagues, mentors, and mentees. Expect to both give and receive productive critique.
    • Communicate schedule changes ≥ 48 hours in advance when possible, especially for husbandry responsibilities.
    • Respond to emails within 24–48 hours during the work week. Let me know when you will be out of town for extended periods in case lab issues come up.
    • You are not expected to respond outside of standard work hours. You may notice emails from me arriving at odd hours; that reflects my schedule, not an expectation of yours. Unless we have agreed in advance that a situation calls for it (a looming proposal deadline, a time-critical milestone), there is no expectation that you will read or reply to messages outside of your standard work hours.
    • Send a summary within 24 hours of every 1-on-1 meeting. This is critical for staying accountable on action items and discussed topics, avoiding redundancy, and keeping the team on track. It is a small habit with a large return.
    • Alert lab members and appropriate staff if something is wrong. Use your best judgment about urgency and who to contact. You are not expected to monitor or manage your peers, but you are expected to speak up when something needs attention.

    Approach and attitude¶

    • Be resourceful. I don't know all the answers and you will often learn more effectively by working things out yourself first. Ask questions. Sometimes I will have an answer, and sometimes I will point you toward useful paths for finding one.
    • Respect my time as I respect yours. If we have a meeting scheduled, be on time, or let me know in advance if you will be late.
    • Attend lab meetings unless a scheduling conflict is communicated in advance.
    • Contribute to shared tasks: cleaning, stocking, and maintaining communal spaces.
    • Mentor junior members as you gain seniority. This is a professional obligation, not optional.
    • Treat all organisms with care. Hatfield has a resident veterinarian who conducts unannounced welfare checks.
    • Be a full participant in this lab community. That means attending lab meetings on time, contributing actively (leading themes, presenting, asking questions), coming to social events, and taking part in outreach when you can. It also means being supportive of your labmates: say hello, help when asked, and be welcoming to newcomers. I expect that you will also tell me when you genuinely cannot do one of these things. You are juggling a lot, and sometimes you have to say no. That is fine.

    Presence and schedule¶

    • Attendance: I generally expect about five days a week, averaging 8–10 hours per day. I do not monitor exact arrival and departure times, but I do expect steady, consistent progress that reflects a standard work week.
    • Atypical hours: I will consider alternative schedules if the project permits, but I strongly encourage daytime hours. They make it much easier to stay connected to the lab community and to get help from staff and peers when you need it.
    • Other responsibilities: Teaching, coursework, and fieldwork are accounted for. If your schedule is affected by these, let me know.

    Authorship and funding¶

    • Discuss authorship before submitting or presenting. Authorship conversations should happen early in every project. Typically, PIs contribute intellectually and financially to student projects and are listed as senior authors. If a project did not involve my effort in those ways, I would not expect authorship, but that conversation needs to happen explicitly, not by assumption.
    • Actively pursue funding to support your research. Learning to write grants and sell your ideas is one of the most important skills you will develop here. It takes practice. I will help, but I need to see the effort coming from you. Future employers in research careers will look at your funding record alongside your publications.

    Your work¶

    • Write every week. Even rough drafts. Writing is thinking, and the habit matters more than the output on any given day.
    • Present your work at lab meeting at least once a year. External conferences count too.
    • Make your code and data open from the start. Version-control everything on the GurrLab GitHub. Open science is not a favor to the community; it is how we hold ourselves accountable.
